How Long Does It Take to Learn Noorani Qaida?

November 2026 · Hafiza Online Academy

It is the first question almost every parent asks: how long will it take my child to finish Noorani Qaida? The honest answer is that it depends — on the child's age, how many classes a week they take, and how much they practise between lessons. There is no single number that fits every student.

What we can give you is a realistic picture. In this guide we explain the factors that set the pace, the timelines most families see, and how to tell whether your child is progressing well — even if it is taking a little longer than you expected.

The short answer

As a general guide, a child taking classes 3 days a week and practising a little each day often finishes Noorani Qaida in a few months. Adults who practise more, or who already know the Arabic alphabet from another language, can move faster. Younger children, or students taking 2 classes a week, may take a little longer — and that is completely fine.

That range is wide on purpose. The Qaida is about 20 to 30 pages, but the time it takes to work through them well depends far more on the student than on the book. A tutor who repeats a lesson until a letter is pronounced correctly is doing the right thing, even if it adds a week or two.

What affects how long it takes?

1. Age

Young children learn sounds very well, but they can only focus for so long. A four- or five-year-old may need more repetition and shorter steps than an eight-year-old, who can concentrate for longer and follow instructions more easily. Older children and adults often move through the early pages faster, because they understand the idea of a vowel mark or a joined letter more quickly.

2. How many classes a week

This is the factor families control most directly. Three days a week is the most popular choice at Hafiza Online Academy because it gives steady progress without overloading a child. Two days a week works well for a gentler start, and five days a week suits families who want faster progress. Roughly speaking, a student taking 5 classes a week will finish noticeably sooner than one taking 2 — but only if the extra lessons do not tire the child out.

3. Practice between lessons

A few minutes of reading practice each day matters more than the number of lessons. Reading is a skill built by repetition. A child who reads the current page aloud for five minutes a day will progress far faster than a child who only reads during the lesson itself. Parents sitting nearby and listening — even without knowing Arabic — make a real difference.

4. Starting point

Some children arrive knowing a few letters, perhaps from hearing family members read, or from a weekend class. Others start from nothing at all. A tutor checks the student's level in the first class and starts from exactly the right page, so no time is wasted on what the child already knows.

5. Difficult letters

A few sounds take longer than the rest. Letters like 'Ain, Ha and Qaaf have no English equivalent, and many children — and adults — need extra weeks of gentle practice before these sounds come naturally. That is normal, and it is not a sign of a problem.

Typical timelines by schedule

These are rough guides, not promises. Every child is different, and a good tutor will never rush a student to meet a date.

  • 5 days a week: the fastest route. A focused older child or adult practising daily can work through the Qaida in a relatively short time.
  • 3 days a week: the most popular choice. Steady, manageable progress — often a few months from the alphabet to the final pages.
  • 2 days a week: a gentler pace. Progress is slower but still steady, and this suits younger children or busy families.

The schedule matters less than the regularity. Three lessons a week, every week, beats five lessons one week and none the next. Holidays and missed weeks will stretch the timeline, and that is fine — the Qaida will still be there when the student returns.

How long does it take for adults?

Adults often surprise themselves. Many adults who never learned to read Arabic, or who learned as children and forgot, move through the Qaida faster than they expect. They can focus for the full 30-minute lesson, they understand the rules quickly, and they can practise on their own. The main challenge for adults is usually the unfamiliar sounds, which need patient repetition — and that is exactly what one-to-one lessons are good for.

Adult students, including many sisters learning from the beginning, often finish the Qaida in a few months of regular lessons. Some take longer, and that is fine too. The goal is correct reading, not a fast finish.

How do I know my child is progressing well?

Progress in the Qaida is not only about page numbers. Here are the signs that matter:

  • They read the current page without being told each letter. They may be slow, but they are reading, not guessing.
  • They correct themselves. A child who stops and fixes their own mistake is learning properly.
  • Old pages stay easy. If your child can still read last month's pages fluently, the learning is sticking.
  • They are willing to come to the lesson. A child who is not afraid of the lesson will learn far faster than one who dreads it.

If progress feels slow, ask the tutor for an update before worrying. At Hafiza Online Academy, parents receive regular updates on what their child is learning and what to practise. Often the tutor is deliberately repeating a page to fix a sound that would cause problems later — and that patience pays off when the child starts reading the Quran.

"There is no prize for rushing. A student who reads the Qaida slowly but correctly will read the Quran far better than one who was hurried through it with shaky pronunciation."

What comes after Noorani Qaida?

Finishing the Qaida is the beginning, not the end. When a student can read its final pages fluently, the tutor moves them on to Nazra — reading the Quran itself, starting from the first pages of the Mushaf. From there, the path continues into Tajweed, where the rules of beautiful and correct recitation are learned in detail, and later, for families who wish it, Hifz, the memorization of the Quran.

Frequently asked questions

How many months does Noorani Qaida usually take?

There is no fixed number. A child taking 3 classes a week and practising a little each day often finishes in a few months. Younger children or students taking 2 classes a week may take longer, and that is completely fine — correct reading matters more than speed.

Will more classes a week make my child finish faster?

Usually, yes — up to a point. Five days a week gives faster progress than two, but only if the child stays fresh and willing. Three days a week is the most popular choice because it balances progress with a child's energy and schoolwork.

My child has been on the same page for weeks. Is something wrong?

Almost certainly not. Tutors repeat a page when a sound is not yet correct — especially difficult letters like 'Ain and Qaaf. Fixing it now saves far more time later than rushing on with a shaky sound. Ask the tutor for an update if you are unsure.

Can adults finish Noorani Qaida faster than children?

Often, yes. Adults can focus for the full lesson, understand the rules quickly, and practise on their own. Many adult students finish the Qaida in a few months of regular lessons.
