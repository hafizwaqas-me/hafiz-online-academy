Common Tajweed Mistakes and How to Fix Them

November 2026 · Hafiza Online Academy

Almost everyone who reads the Quran makes the same handful of mistakes. They are not a sign of carelessness — most of them come from habits learned years ago, or from sounds that do not exist in the reader's first language. The good news is that these mistakes are well known, and each one has a clear fix.

This guide covers the most common Tajweed mistakes we hear in one-to-one lessons, explains why each one happens, and shows how to correct it. If you recognise yourself in any of these, you are in good company — and a qualified tutor can help you fix them one by one.

1. Mixing up similar letters

The most common mistake of all is confusing letters that sound alike to a non-Arabic speaker. Ha and Ha, Seen and Saad, Daal and Dhaad, Ta and Taa, Qaaf and Kaaf — each pair is a different letter, from a different place in the mouth or throat, and each can change a word's meaning.

Why it happens: your ear was trained by your first language. If a sound does not exist in English or Urdu, your brain quietly replaces it with the nearest sound it knows.

How to fix it: learn the Makharij — the exit point of each letter. Practise the two letters side by side, listening to the tutor read each one, then reading them back yourself. Slow, deliberate repetition of the pair, a few minutes a day, retrains the ear far faster than reading whole pages quickly. In a one-to-one lesson, the tutor hears the difference immediately and corrects it in the moment, which is something a recording or a book cannot do.

2. Stretching Madd too long — or not at all

Madd means stretching a vowel sound, and beginners get it wrong in both directions. Some readers stretch every long vowel for the same length, whatever the rule. Others skip the stretch entirely, so words run together and the rhythm of the verse is lost.

Why it happens: Madd is measured in counts — two counts for natural Madd, four or five for some types, six for others — and until your ear learns the counts, the lengths all feel the same.

How to fix it: practise counting. A simple method is to count on your fingers as you stretch: open and close your fingers once for two counts. Read short passages slowly, with the tutor counting along, until the two-count stretch feels natural. Only then move on to the longer Madds, one type at a time. Speed is the enemy here — slow and correct beats fast and wrong every time.

3. Skipping or overdoing the Ghunnah

Ghunnah is the nasal sound that comes through the nose, most famously in the rules of Noon Sakinah and Meem Sakinah. Some readers skip it entirely, reading Ikhfa and Idgham as plain letters. Others do the opposite, adding a nasal hum where the rule calls for a clear reading — most often after the throat letters, where Izhar requires the Noon to be read plainly.

Why it happens: the nasal sound is subtle, and many readers never learned when it applies and when it does not.

How to fix it: learn the four rules of Noon Sakinah and the three rules of Meem Sakinah, and practise each one with examples before moving on. A useful check: gently hold your nose while reading a word that should have Ghunnah — if the sound changes, the Ghunnah is there. If it does not change, the sound is missing and needs work.

4. Rushing through the recitation

Many readers, especially those who learned as children, read far too quickly. Letters blur together, Madds disappear, and the rules of stopping are ignored. The recitation may sound fluent, but it is not correct — and speed hides mistakes that a slower reading would expose.

Why it happens: reading fast feels like reading well, and old habits are hard to break. Children are often praised for finishing a page quickly, which teaches them that speed is the goal.

How to fix it: deliberately slow down. Read at half your usual speed for a few weeks, giving each letter its full sound and each Madd its full length. Read shorter passages — a few lines, not a whole page — and read them well. A tutor who stops you and asks you to repeat a verse slowly is helping, not holding you back.

5. Stopping in the wrong place

The Quran has stopping signs (Waqf) that show where it is good to pause and where it is not. Beginners often stop in the middle of a phrase because they run out of breath, or because they stop at the end of a line rather than at a proper stopping point. Stopping in the wrong place can break the meaning of a verse.

Why it happens: most readers were never taught the Waqf signs, or they stop where it is convenient rather than where it is correct.

How to fix it: learn the main Waqf signs — the small letters and symbols above the text — and what each one means. Plan your breaths before you start a verse: read ahead, find a proper stopping point, and take a breath there. If you must stop mid-phrase because you run out of breath, go back and repeat from the start of the phrase when you resume.

6. Ignoring Qalqalah

Qalqalah is the echoing or bouncing sound made on five letters — Qaaf, Taa, Ba, Jeem and Daal — when they carry a Sukoon. Beginners often read these letters flat, without the bounce, which makes the recitation sound dull and can make the letter hard to hear.

Why it happens: Qalqalah is a small detail that many readers were never taught, and it is easy to miss when reading quickly.

How to fix it: practise the five letters on their own with a Sukoon, exaggerating the bounce at first, until your ear recognises it. Then practise them in words, and then in verses. Once you can hear Qalqalah, you will start noticing it everywhere — and your recitation will sound noticeably clearer.

7. Reading letters with the wrong heaviness

Some Arabic letters are heavy (Mufakhkham) and some are light (Muraqqaq). Beginners often read heavy letters lightly, or make light letters heavy — especially Ra, which is heavy or light depending on its vowel, and Lam in the word Allah, which is heavy after Fatha or Damma and light after Kasra.

Why it happens: heaviness is a quality of sound, not a separate letter, so it is easy to overlook. It is also strongly affected by the reader's first language.

How to fix it: learn which letters are always heavy, which are always light, and which change. Practise the heavy letters — Kha, Saad, Dhaad, Ghayn, Taa, Qaaf and Zaa — until the full, deep sound feels natural, then contrast them with their light neighbours. Listening to a good reciter and imitating the heaviness, one letter at a time, is the fastest way to learn this.

"Every mistake on this list is fixable — and most of them are fixed the same way: slowly, one rule at a time, with someone listening who can hear what you cannot yet hear in your own reading."

Frequently asked questions

Is it too late to fix Tajweed mistakes I learned as a child?

No. Many of our students are adults correcting habits they learned years ago. Old habits take a little time to change, but with one-to-one correction and short daily practice, they do change — usually faster than students expect.

Which Tajweed mistake should I fix first?

Start with the letters themselves — the Makharij. If a letter is pronounced from the wrong place, no other rule will sound right. Once the letters are correct, work through Noon Sakinah, Meem Sakinah and Madd, one rule at a time.

Can I fix Tajweed mistakes by listening to recordings alone?

Listening helps a great deal, but it is usually not enough on its own. You need someone who can hear your recitation and tell you what is wrong, because your own ear has learned to accept your mistakes. A tutor plus listening is the combination that works.

How long does it take to correct these mistakes?

It depends on how many mistakes there are and how often you practise. Most students notice clear improvement within a few weeks of regular one-to-one lessons, and the main rules settle over a few months. The key is regular correction and short daily practice.
