Noorani Qaida for Kids — Complete Beginner's Guide

November 2026 · Hafiza Online Academy

For most Muslim children, the journey to reading the Quran begins with one small book: Noorani Qaida. It is the primer that has taught generations of children the Arabic alphabet, the sounds of each letter, and how to join letters into words. Before a child ever opens the Quran, the Qaida gives them the reading skills they need to do it with confidence.

This guide is written for parents. It explains what Noorani Qaida teaches, when your child should start, what a lesson looks like, and how you can support your child at home — even if you cannot read Arabic yourself.

What is Noorani Qaida?

Noorani Qaida is a short beginner's booklet, usually around 20 to 30 pages, that teaches reading Arabic step by step. It does not teach the meaning of the Quran or its translation. Its one job is reading: recognising letters, pronouncing them correctly, and joining them into words.

The Qaida is arranged in a careful order. Each lesson adds one small new idea on top of what the child already knows, so a complete beginner is never asked to do something they have not been prepared for. A tutor works through it page by page, listening to the child read aloud and correcting mistakes straight away.

Because the method has been used for so long, it is well tested. Children in Pakistan, India, Bangladesh and Muslim communities around the world — including the USA, UK, Canada and Australia — have started with the same book for generations.

What does the Qaida teach your child?

The booklet moves through clear stages. Here is the path your child will follow:

1. The Arabic alphabet

First come the 29 letters of the Arabic alphabet, from Alif to Yaa. Your child learns each letter's name, its shape and its sound. Some letters are easy for English-speaking children — Ba, Ta, Meem sound familiar — while others, like 'Ain, Ha and Qaaf, have no English equivalent and need careful listening and practice.

2. Harakat — the short vowel marks

Next come the harakat: Fatha, Kasra and Damma. These small marks above or below a letter tell the reader which short vowel sound to make. This is the moment reading starts to click, because letters stop being isolated sounds and become syllables.

3. Joining letters into words

Arabic letters change shape depending on where they sit in a word — at the start, in the middle, or at the end. The Qaida teaches these joined forms gradually, so your child moves from reading single letters to reading whole words.

4. Tanween, Sukoon and Shaddah

Later lessons add Tanween (the "n" sound at the end of a word), Sukoon (a letter with no vowel, read plainly) and Shaddah (a doubled letter, read with emphasis). These are the marks a child sees constantly in the Quran, so learning them in the Qaida means there are no surprises later.

5. Madd and basic reading rules

The final lessons introduce Madd — stretching a sound — and the basic rules for stopping and starting. By the end of the Qaida, your child can read simple Arabic words and short sentences with reasonable fluency, and is ready to begin Nazra, reading the Quran itself.

When should a child start?

Most children can start Noorani Qaida once they can sit and focus for a short lesson, often from around 4 or 5 years old. There is no exact age that fits every child. Some four-year-olds are ready; some five-year-olds are not quite there yet. A good tutor can tell in the first class whether a child is ready, or whether it is better to wait a few months.

Starting early is not the same as starting well. A child who begins at five and learns each letter correctly will read the Quran far better than a child who was pushed in at three and rushed through with shaky pronunciation. The right time to start is when your child can enjoy the lesson, not when they are forced through it.

It is also never too late. Older children who start at seven, eight or nine often move through the Qaida quickly because they can focus for longer and understand instructions more easily.

What does a lesson look like?

At Hafiza Online Academy, Noorani Qaida lessons are live and one-to-one, on Zoom, Skype or WhatsApp — whichever your family prefers. Each lesson is 30 minutes, one child with one tutor. The tutor shares the Qaida page on screen, the child reads aloud, and the tutor corrects pronunciation immediately.

This is the great advantage of one-to-one teaching: in a group, a child can hide a mispronounced letter for weeks. One-to-one, every mistake is heard and fixed in the moment. The tutor also adjusts the pace to your child — repeating a page when it is needed, or moving on when the child is ready.

Between lessons, your child practises the current page for a few minutes a day. Short daily practice matters far more than one long session at the weekend. For young children, a parent sitting nearby and listening — even without knowing Arabic — makes a real difference, because the child knows someone is paying attention.

Tips for parents

  • Keep lessons regular. Three short lessons a week beat one long lesson. Little and often is how children learn sounds.
  • Sit nearby for young children. You do not need to know Arabic — just being present keeps a young child focused, and it keeps the lesson safe.
  • Praise effort, not speed. A child who is praised for trying will keep trying. Comparing them to a sibling or cousin only slows them down.
  • Make practice a small daily habit. A few minutes after school, or before bed, works better than an hour once a week.
  • Ask the tutor for a progress update. At Hafiza Online Academy, parents receive regular updates on what their child is learning and what to practise.
  • Be patient with difficult letters. Sounds like 'Ain and Qaaf take time. Gentle repetition, not pressure, is what fixes them.

Most children can start once they can sit and focus for a short lesson, often from around 4 or 5 years old. The tutor adjusts the pace and lesson length to the child's age, and it is fine to wait a few months if your child is not ready yet.

Does my child need to know Arabic before starting?

No. Noorani Qaida is designed for complete beginners. It starts with the alphabet itself, and no knowledge of Arabic — spoken or written — is needed.
