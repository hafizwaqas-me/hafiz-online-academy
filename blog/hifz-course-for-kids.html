Hifz Course for Kids — How It Works

November 2026 · Hafiza Online Academy

Many parents dream of their child becoming a Hafiz — someone who has memorized the whole Quran. It is a beautiful goal, and children are often very good at it: a young mind absorbs new words and sounds quickly, and a habit built in childhood can last a lifetime. But Hifz is also a long journey, and parents rightly want to know how it actually works before they enrol their child.

This guide explains how a Hifz course for kids works at Hafiza Online Academy: what your child needs before starting, what a lesson looks like, how revision keeps the memorization strong, and how families fit Hifz around school and daily life.

What does a child need before starting Hifz?

The most important requirement is fluent reading. A child should be able to read the Quran fluently with correct Tajweed before they start memorizing it. That is why most children begin with Noorani Qaida, then Nazra (reading the Quran), and then Tajweed, before moving on to Hifz. Trying to memorize from a text you cannot yet read fluently makes every lesson harder than it needs to be.

There is no strict starting age for Hifz, but children usually begin once they can read fluently and can sit and focus for a full lesson. Our Hifz lessons are 45 minutes long, which is longer than our 30-minute lessons for other courses, because memorization and revision take more time. The tutor will tell you honestly in the trial classes whether your child is ready, or whether a little more time on reading would help them more. Your child does not need to understand Arabic to memorize the Quran — Hifz is about memorizing the Arabic text accurately, word by word.

What does a Hifz lesson look like?

Hifz lessons at Hafiza Online Academy are live and one-to-one, on Zoom, Skype or WhatsApp — whichever your family prefers. Your child meets the same certified tutor each time, so the tutor knows exactly where they are in the Quran and how they learn best.

1. Revision first

Every lesson starts with revision, not with new memorization. The tutor listens to the child recite what they memorized recently, and some of what they memorized earlier. This is the most important part of the lesson: new memorization without revision fades quickly, while revision is what turns a new lesson into lasting memory.

2. New lesson — Sabaq

Next comes the Sabaq, the new portion to memorize. The tutor reads it aloud first, verse by verse, with correct Tajweed, and the child repeats each verse after the tutor. The new portion is kept small — often just a few lines — because a small portion learned well is worth far more than a long portion learned badly.

3. Reading and correcting

The child then reads the new portion back to the tutor several times. The tutor corrects pronunciation, Tajweed and any missed words on the spot, and helps the child connect the verses so they flow together. The lesson ends with the child reciting the new Sabaq from memory, or as close to it as they can manage.

4. Practice at home

Between lessons, the child practises the new Sabaq and the revision the tutor set. Short, regular practice works far better than one long session before the next lesson. Even fifteen or twenty minutes a day, with a parent listening nearby, makes a real difference.

How does the revision system work?

Hifz is built on a simple three-part revision system that has been used for generations: Sabaq, Sabqi and Manzil. Sabaq is the new lesson memorized today. Sabqi is the revision of the recent lessons — the last few days or the current section. Manzil is the revision of everything memorized long ago, recited regularly so that older portions stay strong. You can read a full explanation in our article on Sabaq, Sabqi and Manzil.

Parents sometimes worry when the pace of new memorization seems to slow down. It usually slows because the tutor is giving revision the time it deserves. A child who memorizes quickly but forgets quickly has not really gained anything. A steady pace, with strong revision, is what produces a Hafiz whose memorization lasts.

Where do children start memorizing?

Many children begin with Juz Amma, the 30th Juz, which contains the short surahs at the end of the Quran. The short verses give a child quick wins and confidence, and the surahs are ones they already hear often in prayer. From there, children usually work backwards through the shorter Juz, or start from the beginning of the Quran, depending on the tutor's plan and the family's preference.

There is no single right order for everyone. What matters is that the order is consistent, the child knows where they are, and the tutor keeps a clear record of what has been memorized and revised. Our article on how to memorize Juz Amma explains the usual starting point in more detail.

How do families fit Hifz around school?

Hifz works best when it becomes part of the daily routine rather than an extra burden. Most families choose 5 days a week for Hifz, because daily contact with the Quran keeps both new memorization and revision fresh. Our Hifz plan is 5 days a week for $65 per month, with 45-minute lessons. Families who want a gentler start can talk to us about fewer days — the tutor will adjust the pace to match.

Lesson times are flexible, and because classes are online, families in the USA, UK, Canada, Australia and worldwide can choose times that fit their own timezone and their child's school day. Many children take their lesson after school, or before school in the morning when their mind is fresh. A regular time each day helps the habit stick.

Parents play a big part, especially for younger children. You do not need to know Arabic yourself — sitting nearby, making sure the child practises, and praising their effort all help enormously.

"Hifz is not a race. A child who memorizes a little each day, revises it well, and keeps going will reach the end — and, more importantly, will still remember it when they get there."

Tips for parents of Hifz students

  • Protect the revision. New lessons are exciting, but revision is what keeps the Quran in memory. Make sure practice time includes the older portions, not just today's Sabaq.
  • Keep it daily. A short practice every day beats a long practice once a week. Little and often is how memorization becomes permanent.
  • Praise effort and consistency. Some days the Sabaq will be easy and some days it will be hard. Praise the child for showing up and trying, not just for perfect recitation.
  • Talk to the tutor. Ask for regular updates on what your child has memorized and what needs more revision. If the pace feels too fast or too slow, say so — the plan can be adjusted.
  • Use a female tutor if that suits your family. We have certified female tutors for kids and sisters, at no extra cost — just ask when you book.
