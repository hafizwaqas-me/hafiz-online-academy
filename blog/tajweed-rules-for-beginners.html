Tajweed Rules for Beginners

November 2026 · Hafiza Online Academy

Tajweed is the set of rules for reciting the Quran correctly and beautifully — pronouncing every letter from its proper place, giving each sound its full right, and pausing and stretching exactly where the rules require. The word itself comes from an Arabic root meaning to make something good or beautiful, and that is what Tajweed does for recitation.

If you can already read the Quran but you know your pronunciation could be better, this guide is for you. It explains the most important beginner rules — Makharij, the rules of Noon Sakinah and Meem Sakinah, and Madd — in simple language, with no previous knowledge assumed.

What is Tajweed, and why does it matter?

The Quran was revealed in Arabic, and its words carry precise meanings. Changing a letter's sound can change a word's meaning, so correct pronunciation is not a decoration — it is part of reading the Quran properly. Tajweed gives you the rules to do that.

Many people learned to read the Quran as children and never studied Tajweed properly. Their reading is fluent, but some letters are pronounced from the wrong place, or sounds are merged when they should be kept separate. Learning Tajweed as an adult — or teaching it to a child after Noorani Qaida and Nazra — fixes these habits one by one. It is never too late to start, and the improvement is usually noticeable within weeks.

Makharij — where each letter comes from

Makharij (singular: Makhraj) means the exit points of the letters — the place in the mouth or throat where each letter is produced. There are five main areas:

  • The throat (Al-Halq): the letters Hamza, Ha, 'Ain, Ha, Ghayn and Kha come from the throat, at three depths — the bottom, the middle and the top.
  • The tongue (Al-Lisan): most letters come from the tongue — Qaaf from the back, Kaaf a little further forward, and the rest from the middle, the sides and the tip of the tongue against the teeth and gums.
  • The lips (Ash-Shafatan): Ba, Meem and Waw come from the lips, and Fa from the lower lip against the upper teeth.
  • The nose (Al-Khayshoom): the nasal sound, the Ghunnah, comes through the nose — it is part of the rules of Noon and Meem, explained below.
  • The empty space of the mouth (Al-Jawf): the long vowel sounds — Alif, Waw and Yaa when they are used as Madd letters — come from the hollow of the mouth.

Why does this matter? Because several Arabic letters sound similar to an English speaker but come from different places. Ha and Ha are not the same letter. Qaaf and Kaaf are not the same letter. Learning the Makharij is how you learn to tell them apart — and how a listener can tell that you are reading each letter correctly. A good tutor listens for the place of each letter and corrects it gently, one letter at a time.

Noon Sakinah and Tanween — four rules

Noon Sakinah is a Noon with a Sukoon (no vowel), and Tanween is the double vowel mark that ends with an "n" sound. Whenever one of these appears, the way you read it depends on the letter that follows. There are four rules:

1. Izhar — clear pronunciation

When the next letter is one of the six throat letters (Hamza, Ha, 'Ain, Ha, Ghayn, Kha), the Noon is read clearly and plainly, with no nasal sound. The Noon is simply pronounced as it is.

2. Idgham — merging

When the next letter is one of the six letters of the word Yarmaloon (Yaa, Ra, Meem, Lam, Waw, Noon), the Noon merges into the following letter. With Yaa, Noon, Meem and Waw, the merge carries a nasal sound (Ghunnah). With Lam and Ra, the merge is without Ghunnah.

3. Iqlab — changing

When the next letter is Ba, the Noon sound changes into a Meem sound, read with a Ghunnah. This is the only letter that causes this change, which makes it easy to remember.

4. Ikhfa — hiding

When the next letter is any of the remaining fifteen letters, the Noon is read with a light nasal sound, partly hidden — the tongue moves towards the next letter without quite touching it. This is the most common of the four rules, and it takes practice to get the sound right.

These four rules appear on almost every page of the Quran. Once you learn them, you will start noticing them everywhere — and your recitation will immediately sound more correct.

Meem Sakinah — three rules

Meem Sakinah is a Meem with a Sukoon. Its rules are simpler than Noon's, because there are only three:

1. Ikhfa Shafawi — hiding at the lips

When the next letter is Ba, the Meem is read with a light nasal sound, with the lips coming close together without pressing fully. This is the lip version of Ikhfa.

2. Idgham Shafawi — merging at the lips

When the next letter is another Meem, the two Meems merge into one, read with a Ghunnah. The lips do the merging, which is why it is called Shafawi — of the lips.

3. Izhar Shafawi — clear pronunciation

When the next letter is any other letter, the Meem is read clearly and plainly. The only point to watch is with Waw and Fa, where the reader must be careful not to accidentally hide the Meem — it should stay clear.

Madd — stretching the sound

Madd means stretching a vowel sound. The three Madd letters are Alif (after a Fatha), Waw (after a Damma) and Yaa (after a Kasra). The basic rule, Madd Tabee'i (natural Madd), is to stretch the sound for two counts — about the time it takes to open and close your fingers.

Other kinds of Madd stretch for longer: four or five counts, or six counts, depending on what follows the Madd letter — a Hamza, a letter with a Shaddah, or a Sukoon. A beginner does not need to memorise every type at once. The important first step is to stretch where there is a Madd and not to stretch where there is not. Over-stretching and under-stretching are among the most common beginner mistakes, and a tutor will help you hear the difference.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to know Tajweed before I can read the Quran?

No. You can begin reading the Quran after Noorani Qaida, and learn Tajweed as you go. Many students learn the rules gradually while continuing their Nazra reading, which is often the best way — the rules make sense when you meet them in real verses.

How long does it take to learn basic Tajweed?

It depends on how often you take lessons and how much you practise. Most students learn the main rules — Makharij, Noon Sakinah, Meem Sakinah and basic Madd — over a few months of regular one-to-one lessons, and then keep refining them for much longer.

Can children learn Tajweed?

Yes. Children usually start Tajweed after finishing Noorani Qaida and gaining some fluency in Nazra. The rules are taught simply, one at a time, with plenty of listening and repetition.

Can I learn Tajweed if I learned to read the Quran years ago?

Yes, and many of our students do exactly that. A tutor will listen to your recitation, find the habits that need correcting, and work through the rules with you one by one. Old habits take a little time to change, but they do change.
