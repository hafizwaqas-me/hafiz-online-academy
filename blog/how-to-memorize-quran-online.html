How to Memorize the Quran Online — Complete Hifz Guide

November 2026 · Hafiz Online Academy

Memorising the Quran — Hifz — is one of the most rewarding goals a Muslim can set. It is also one of the most demanding. A person who completes it carries the entire Quran in their heart, and that achievement stays with them for life. But Hifz is not a short project, and it is not something to begin without understanding how it works.

This guide explains what Hifz is, who it suits, the traditional system of daily lessons and revision that makes it possible, and how online Hifz classes work in practice.

What is Hifz?

Hifz means committing the entire Quran — all 114 Surahs, 6,236 verses — to memory, so that it can be recited from memory, without looking at the text. A person who has done this is called a Hafiz (for a man or boy) or a Hafiza (for a woman or girl).

Hifz is not the same as reading fluently. A fluent reader still needs the Mushaf in front of them. A Hafiz has internalised the words so deeply that they can be recalled in order, accurately, at any time. That depth of memory is built slowly, verse by verse, through a system that has been used for centuries.

Who is Hifz for?

Hifz suits students who can already read the Quran fluently, with reasonable Tajweed. That is the usual starting point: a student finishes Noorani Qaida, reads through the Quran in Nazra, learns the basics of Tajweed, and then begins memorisation.

Children often begin Hifz between the ages of 7 and 12, when memory is strong and school schedules can accommodate daily classes — but adults begin Hifz too, and many complete it. What matters is not age, but readiness: fluent reading, a realistic daily routine, and the patience to keep going for years.

It is worth being honest with yourself before starting. Hifz asks for daily effort over a long period. A student who starts with excitement but cannot keep a daily routine will struggle. A student who starts modestly — a few verses a day, every day — will steadily get there.

The Sabaq, Sabqi and Manzil system

The traditional Hifz system has three parts, and every day's work includes all three. Understanding them is the key to understanding Hifz itself.

Sabaq — the new lesson

Sabaq is the new portion memorised each day. For a beginner, this may be just a few verses. As the student progresses, the daily Sabaq grows — often to around half a page or a full page of the Mushaf per day, depending on the student's ability and schedule. The student memorises the new portion before the lesson, then recites it to the tutor, who corrects any mistake immediately.

Sabqi — recent revision

Sabqi is the revision of what was memorised recently — usually the last few days' lessons, or the current Juz (part) in progress. The student recites it to the tutor each day. This is what stops new memorisation from fading: a verse learned last week is still fresh enough to be strengthened, but only if it is revisited.

Manzil — older revision

Manzil is the revision of everything memorised long ago — the earlier Juz that were completed months or even years before. The student works through their older memorisation on a rotating cycle, so that no part of the Quran is ever left unrevised for too long. This is the part students find hardest, and the part that matters most: without Manzil, a Hafiz slowly loses what they worked so hard to learn.

Together, the three parts mean that every lesson looks forward (new verses), looks back a little (recent verses), and looks back a long way (old verses). That balance is what makes complete memorisation possible.

A realistic daily routine

A Hifz student's day usually includes:

  • Memorising the new Sabaq — reading the new verses repeatedly, then reciting them from memory, until they are firm. This is usually done before the lesson, and it is the part that takes the most concentration.
  • The live lesson — at Hafiz Online Academy, Hifz classes are 45 minutes, 5 days a week. In the lesson, the student recites the Sabaq, Sabqi and Manzil to the tutor, who listens and corrects.
  • Revision during the day — short revision sessions, for example after prayers, keep the day's work fresh. Many students revise while walking, or recite to a parent or sibling.
  • Listening to recitation — listening to a good Qari recite the portion being memorised helps the words settle. It is especially useful for the new Sabaq.

The exact routine is set with the tutor, around the student's school or work and their family's schedule.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Memorising too much, too fast. A large Sabaq that is memorised weakly is worse than a small Sabaq memorised firmly. Speed is not the goal — retention is.
  • Skipping revision. Every student is tempted to push ahead with new verses and neglect Manzil. It always catches up with them. Revision is not optional; it is half the work.
  • Memorising without understanding the words. You do not need to be fluent in Arabic, but knowing the general meaning of the verses you memorise makes them far easier to remember and far harder to mix up.
  • Learning alone, without a teacher. Memorisation without a tutor leads to mistakes that become fixed in memory. A tutor hears errors the student cannot hear themselves — and in Hifz, an error memorised is an error that is very hard to remove.
  • Stopping and starting. Long breaks are the enemy of Hifz. Even a short daily session during holidays or busy weeks keeps the memorisation alive.

"In Hifz, revision is not something you do after memorising. Revision is the memorising — it is what turns words you once knew into words you will never forget."

How long does full Hifz take?

For most students, full Hifz takes years. A dedicated student memorising a page a day, five days a week, with consistent revision, will still need several years to complete all 30 Juz — and that assumes no long breaks and steady revision throughout. Children in full-time Hifz programmes may finish sooner; adults and school-going children usually take longer. That is completely normal.

Be cautious of anyone who promises full Hifz in a few months. It is far better to plan for years, memorise firmly, and arrive at the end with the Quran truly in your heart — than to rush and arrive with weak, fading memorisation. Your tutor will set a pace that fits your life, and will adjust it as you progress.

How do online Hifz classes work?

Online Hifz classes at Hafiz Online Academy are live, one-to-one, on Zoom, Skype or WhatsApp — whichever the student prefers. The student recites to a certified Hafiz or Hafiza tutor, who listens to every verse and corrects mistakes on the spot. Learning Hifz online works well because the core of Hifz is listening: the tutor listens to the student, and the student listens to correct recitation. A video call carries that perfectly. Parents can also sit in on lessons for younger children, and they receive regular progress updates on how much their child has memorised and how strong the revision is.
