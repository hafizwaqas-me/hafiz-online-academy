Sabaq, Sabqi and Manzil — the Hifz Revision System Explained

November 2026 · Hafiza Online Academy

If you have ever listened to a Hifz student talk about their lesson, you have probably heard three words: Sabaq, Sabqi and Manzil. They are the heart of how the Quran is memorized and — just as importantly — how it is kept in memory. Many parents hear the words but never get a clear explanation of what each one means, or why all three matter.

This guide explains the three parts simply, shows how they fit together in a daily lesson, and explains what happens when one of them is neglected. If your child is starting Hifz, or you are thinking about it, understanding this system will help you support them far better.

The simple idea behind the system

Memorizing the Quran is not one job — it is two jobs. The first is learning something new. The second is not forgetting what you have already learned. The second job is harder, because memory fades. A verse you memorized perfectly last week can feel shaky today if you have not repeated it since.

The Sabaq, Sabqi and Manzil system divides the second job into two parts — recent revision and old revision — so that nothing is left to fade. Every Hifz lesson touches all three parts: a new lesson, revision of recent lessons, and revision of older lessons. That daily rhythm, repeated over months and years, is what turns memorization into lasting knowledge.

1. Sabaq — the new lesson

Sabaq is the new portion the student memorizes today. It is usually small — a few lines, or a few verses — because the goal is to learn it well, not to cover a lot of ground. The tutor reads the Sabaq aloud first, with correct Tajweed, and the student repeats each verse after the tutor. Then the student reads it back several times, joining the verses together until they can recite the whole portion from memory.

The size of the Sabaq depends on the student. A young child or a new student might take just a few lines a day. A more experienced student with strong revision habits might take half a page or more. The tutor adjusts the size based on how well the student is coping — if the Sabaq is shaky, the next day's Sabaq will be smaller. If it is strong, it may grow a little.

Today's Sabaq becomes tomorrow's Sabqi. That is the first rule of the system: nothing is memorized once and then forgotten. Everything new is revised the very next day, and for several days after that, until it is firm.

2. Sabqi — revision of recent lessons

Sabqi is the revision of what was memorized recently — the last few days of Sabaq, or the current section of the Quran the student is working through. In practice, the tutor will ask the student to recite the Sabaq from the last few days, often as one continuous passage, to check that the new verses are joining together properly.

Sabqi does two important jobs. First, it catches mistakes early. A word misremembered on day one, if it is not corrected, can become a habit that is hard to break. Reciting the recent lessons to the tutor each day means mistakes are heard and fixed while they are still fresh. Second, Sabqi builds fluency. Reciting several days of lessons together teaches the student how the verses flow into each other, so the memorization becomes a smooth recitation rather than a collection of separate fragments.

As the student progresses through a Juz, the Sabqi grows. By the time they are near the end of a Juz, their Sabqi may be a large part of that Juz — and when they finish the Juz, the whole Juz may be recited as Sabqi for a while, to make it firm before they move on.

3. Manzil — revision of old memorization

Manzil is the revision of everything memorized long ago — the Juz and sections completed weeks or months earlier. This is the part that protects the whole journey. Without Manzil, a student can memorize Juz after Juz and then discover, months later, that the early Juz have faded badly.

Manzil works on a cycle. The student recites a set amount of old memorization each day — for example, a Juz a day, or half a Juz a day — working through all the completed Juz in turn, and then starting the cycle again. When a student has memorized only a few Juz, the Manzil cycle is short and the whole of their old memorization is covered every few days. When a student has memorized many Juz, the cycle is longer, and each Juz comes round less often — which is why the Manzil portion of the lesson grows as the student progresses.

How the three parts fit into one lesson

A typical 45-minute Hifz lesson at Hafiza Online Academy follows the same order every day: Manzil first, then Sabqi, then Sabaq. Revision comes before new memorization, because revision protects what is already there. The balance shifts over time: in the first weeks, there is little Manzil to revise, so most of the lesson is Sabaq. As the student memorizes more, Manzil and Sabqi take up more of the lesson, and the new Sabaq becomes a smaller part of it.

Between lessons, the student practises in the same order: Manzil, then Sabqi, then the new Sabaq. Parents can help by asking the child to recite their Manzil to them — even without knowing Arabic, you can follow along in a Mushaf, or simply listen for confidence and fluency. A child who recites their Manzil smoothly is a child whose memorization is strong.

Each part covers a weakness of the others, so skipping one creates a problem the others cannot fix: without Sabaq there is no progress, without Sabqi new lessons never become firm, and without Manzil the early Juz quietly fade and have to be rebuilt months later. That is why a good Hifz tutor is strict about all three parts, every day. The system only works when it is complete.

"Sabaq is what you learn today. Sabqi is what you learned this week. Manzil is everything you have ever learned. A Hafiz is not someone who memorized the Quran once — it is someone who revises it every day."

How parents can help with each part

  • For Sabaq: listen to the new lesson the evening before the class, if you can. A short listen-through often turns a shaky Sabaq into a confident one.
  • For Sabqi: ask your child to recite the last few days of lessons together, as one passage. If they stumble at the joins between days, that is where extra practice helps.
  • Ask the tutor for the plan. Ask which Juz are in Manzil this week, and how the cycle is going. Knowing the plan helps you support it at home.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between Sabqi and Manzil?

Sabqi is revision of recent lessons — the last few days, or the current section you are working through. Manzil is revision of older memorization — Juz completed weeks or months ago, recited on a regular cycle so they stay strong.

How much Manzil should a student recite each day?

It depends on how much they have memorized. A student with a few Juz might cover all of them every few days, while a student with many Juz works through them on a longer cycle. The tutor sets the daily Manzil amount and adjusts it as the student progresses.

Why does my child's new Sabaq seem smaller than before?

Because revision takes up more of the lesson as the student memorizes more. The tutor reduces the new Sabaq so there is enough time for Sabqi and Manzil. This is normal and healthy — it protects everything already memorized.
