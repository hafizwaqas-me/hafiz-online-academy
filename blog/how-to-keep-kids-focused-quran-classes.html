How to Keep Kids Focused in Online Quran Classes

November 2026 · Hafiza Online Academy

Keeping a child focused on a screen for 30 minutes is hard — every parent who has tried online learning knows it. The television is in the next room, a sibling is playing nearby, and the lesson is competing with everything else in the house. But focus in an online Quran class is not a matter of luck. It comes from a few simple habits that parents can set up before the lesson even starts.

The good news is that one-to-one Quran lessons already have a big advantage: the tutor is speaking directly to your child, not to a class of twenty. When the tutor says "read this line", there is no one else to hide behind. With the right setup at home, most children settle into their lessons quickly and stay focused for the full 30 minutes.

Why children lose focus online

Before fixing the problem, it helps to understand it. Children rarely lose focus because they dislike the Quran. They lose focus because the setting works against them. The most common reasons are simple: the lesson time clashes with tiredness, the room is noisy, the device has other apps one tap away, or the child does not know what is expected of them.

Young children also focus in short bursts. A 6-year-old who listens well for ten minutes, drifts for two, and comes back is not failing — that is normal. A good tutor expects this and builds the lesson around it, switching between listening, repeating, pointing and revising so the child is never doing one thing for too long.

The tips below work together. No single one is a magic fix, but put together they turn a distracted lesson into a calm, focused one.

1. Keep the same time every day

Children focus better when they know what is coming. If the Quran lesson is always at the same time — say, 5pm on Monday, Wednesday and Friday — the child stops negotiating and starts expecting it. The lesson becomes part of the day, like school or dinner, instead of a surprise that interrupts play.

Choose the time carefully. The best time is when your child is alert but not wound up: after a snack and a short rest following school, for example, rather than late in the evening when they are tired. Avoid scheduling lessons right after a big meal or right before bedtime. If the current time is not working, ask to change it — a small change in timing often fixes a big focus problem.

2. Set up one quiet corner

Your child does not need a special room, but they do need one consistent spot. A chair at a table in a quiet corner, with the device at eye level, is enough. The key is that the spot is used only for lessons, so sitting there becomes a signal: now it is learning time.

Keep the space clear. Put toys, games and snacks out of sight before the lesson starts. Turn the television off in nearby rooms. If siblings are home, give them something to do elsewhere during the 30 minutes. A child who can see a toy will think about the toy — that is not bad behaviour, it is just how children work.

Good lighting and a working microphone matter too. If the tutor cannot hear your child clearly, the lesson slows down, the child gets bored, and focus drops. Test the device, the internet and the sound before the first lesson, and check them again if anything changes.

3. Remove distractions from the device

Most focus problems on a tablet or laptop come from the device itself. Close every app except the one used for the lesson — Zoom, Skype or WhatsApp, whichever your family uses. Turn off notifications. If the device is shared with other family members, sign out of games and video apps before the lesson, or use a separate profile if the device allows it.

For younger children, a larger screen helps. A laptop or tablet is easier to focus on than a small phone, because the Qaida page is big enough to read without squinting. If a phone is the only option, prop it up on a stand so the child is not holding it — a child holding a phone will fidget with the phone.

4. Sit nearby — especially for young children

For children under 8 or so, a parent sitting nearby is the single most effective focus tool there is. You do not need to know Arabic, and you do not need to teach anything. Your presence tells the child that this lesson matters, and it quietly stops the drifting, the fidgeting and the wandering off.

Sit where your child can see you, but out of the camera's way if you prefer. Listen to the lesson. When the tutor asks your child to practise a page during the week, you will know exactly which page it is and how it should sound. As the child grows older and more confident, you can gradually sit further away.

One caution: resist the urge to correct your child during the lesson. If you jump in every time they misread a letter, the child starts looking at you instead of the tutor, and the lesson becomes a three-way conversation. Let the tutor correct. Your job is to be present, calm and encouraging.

5. Make practice short and daily

Focus in the lesson is much easier when the material is not brand new every time. A child who practised the current page for five minutes the day before will read it with confidence. A child who has not seen it since last week will struggle — and struggling children switch off.

Keep practice short. Five to ten minutes a day is enough for most children. Ask them to read the page they are on, listen, and praise the effort. You do not need to know whether the reading is correct — the tutor will check that in the next lesson. What matters is that the child has looked at the page recently and feels familiar with it.

A simple routine helps: same time, same page, same short practice. Many families practise right after the lesson or at the same time each evening. Tie it to something that already happens — after dinner, before bedtime stories — and it becomes a habit rather than a chore.

6. Praise effort, not perfection

Children focus better when they feel safe to make mistakes. If every misread letter brings a sigh or a correction from the family, the child will start to dread the lesson. If effort brings praise, the child will keep trying.

Notice the small wins: a letter read correctly that was wrong last week, a full line read without stopping, a lesson finished without a complaint. Tell the child you noticed. Tell the tutor too — a tutor who knows what to praise can use it in the next lesson.

Never compare your child with a sibling, cousin or classmate. Children learn at different speeds, and comparison is the fastest way to make a child stop trying. The only fair comparison is with their own reading last month.

7. Talk to the tutor when focus drops

If your child's focus has dropped for a few lessons in a row, tell the tutor. A good tutor can change the pace, vary the activities, move the lesson to a better time, or slow down and revise instead of pushing forward. Sometimes the fix is as simple as starting the lesson with revision the child already knows, so they begin with confidence instead of struggle.

At Hafiza Online Academy, parents receive regular updates on what their child is learning. Use those updates — ask the tutor what to practise, what your child finds hard, and what is going well. The tutor sees your child three times a week; you see them every day. Between you, you have the full picture.

"A focused child is rarely a strict child. More often, it is a child whose lesson comes at the right time, in a quiet corner, with a parent nearby and a tutor who knows their name."

What about older children?

Older children, from around 9 or 10, can usually manage a lesson on their own. The same habits still help — a regular time, a quiet spot, a device with nothing else open — but the parent's role shifts from sitting beside them to checking in afterwards. Ask what they learned, ask them to read you one line, and check in with the tutor if progress stalls.

Teenagers may need a different kind of motivation. Let them choose their own lesson time, and let them talk to the tutor directly about their goals — finishing Nazra, improving Tajweed, or starting Hifz. A student who owns their learning focuses far better than one who feels it is imposed on them.

Frequently asked questions

How long can a child focus in an online Quran class?

Most children focus well for a 30-minute one-to-one lesson, because the tutor works in short bursts — listening, repeating, pointing and revising. Younger children may drift briefly and come back, which is normal.

Should a parent sit with the child during the lesson?

Yes, for young children. A parent sitting nearby keeps the child settled and focused. You do not need to know Arabic — your presence is enough. Older children can usually manage on their own.

What if my child keeps getting distracted every lesson?

Check the basics first: lesson time, quiet space, and other apps closed on the device. Then talk to the tutor — they can change the pace, vary the activities, or move the lesson to a better time.

How much should my child practise between lessons?

Five to ten minutes a day is enough for most children. Short, daily practice keeps the current page familiar, so the next lesson starts with confidence instead of struggle.
