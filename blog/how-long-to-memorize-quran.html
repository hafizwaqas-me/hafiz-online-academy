How Long Does It Take to Memorize the Whole Quran?

November 2026 · Hafiza Online Academy

"How long will it take?" is the first question almost every family asks before starting Hifz, and it is a fair question. You are committing to a long journey, and you want to know what you are committing to. The honest answer is that it varies widely — from under two years to many years — and it depends far more on how much time you can give each day than on how clever you are.

This article gives realistic ranges as general guidance only. There is no exact formula, and any academy that promises you a precise finish date is not being honest with you. What we can do is explain the factors that matter, and show you what typical timelines look like for different kinds of students.

First, how big is the task?

The Quran has 114 surahs, 30 Juz (parts), and 6,236 verses. That sounds enormous — and it is — but it helps to think of it in smaller pieces. One Juz is roughly 20 pages of the standard Mushaf. A student who memorizes half a page a day, five days a week, covers about two and a half pages a week. At that steady pace, the maths is simple, but the real challenge is not the maths: it is keeping the earlier pages strong while you add new ones.

That is why revision matters as much as new memorization. A student who races through new lessons without revising will forget the earlier Juz and have to re-learn them. A student who revises properly — Sabaq, Sabqi and Manzil every day — builds memorization that lasts. The time ranges below assume a proper balance of new lessons and revision, not a race through the text.

What affects how long Hifz takes?

1. How much time you give each day

This is the biggest factor by far. A full-time student who spends several hours a day on Hifz will finish far faster than a child who takes one 45-minute lesson a day alongside school. Neither path is better — they simply fit different lives — but the difference in time is large.

2. Age

Children often memorize quickly because their minds absorb new sounds easily and they have fewer competing demands. Adults can memorize just as well, and often bring stronger motivation and discipline, but they usually have work, family and other commitments that limit their daily time. Age helps, but consistency matters more.

3. Reading fluency and Tajweed

A student who reads fluently with correct Tajweed memorizes faster, because they are not struggling with the words themselves. That is why we recommend completing Noorani Qaida and Nazra first. Time spent on fluent reading before Hifz is never wasted — it pays for itself many times over during memorization.

4. Revision habits

Students who revise every day keep their memorization strong and rarely need to go back and re-learn old lessons. Students who skip revision often find that old Juz fade, and the time spent re-learning them adds months to the journey. Daily revision is the single best way to keep the total time down.

5. Consistency over months and years

Hifz is a marathon, not a sprint. Breaks — holidays, illness, exam periods — are normal, and a good tutor plans around them. But long, unplanned breaks slow progress more than anything else, because memorization fades without regular contact. A steady routine, even a modest one, beats bursts of intense effort followed by long gaps.

Realistic timelines — general guidance

With those factors in mind, here are the broad ranges families usually ask about. Treat them as rough guidance, not promises. Every student is different, and your tutor will give you a clearer picture once they have worked with you for a few weeks.

Full-time students

A full-time student — for example, a child in a dedicated Hifz programme who spends most of the school day on memorization and revision — can often complete the whole Quran in around two to three years. Some finish faster, some take longer, but this is the range families usually hear about for full-time study.

Children memorizing part-time

Most of our students are children memorizing part-time, alongside school, with a daily 45-minute lesson and practice at home. For these children, the journey usually takes several years — often in the region of four to seven years, depending on how many days a week they study and how consistent their revision is. That may sound long, but it is a normal, healthy pace for a child who is also going to school, and the memorization built this way tends to be strong.

Adults memorizing part-time

Adults studying part-time — perhaps one lesson a day, or a few lessons a week around work and family — should expect a longer journey still, often many years. That is completely normal. Many adults memorize a Juz or two a year and are happy with steady, lasting progress. The goal is not speed; it is memorization that stays with you.

How to think about progress

It helps to measure progress in Juz, not in months. Finishing your first Juz is a real achievement. Finishing five Juz means you have memorized a sixth of the Quran. Each Juz you complete is a milestone worth celebrating, and celebrating milestones keeps a child motivated over a long journey.

It also helps to set process goals rather than date goals. Instead of "finish in three years," try "memorize half a page a day, revise every day, and never miss a lesson without a good reason." Process goals are in your control every day. Date goals are not — life gets in the way — and missing a date goal can feel like failure even when your memorization is actually going well.

Your tutor can help here. At Hafiza Online Academy, Hifz tutors keep a record of what each student has memorized and revised, and give parents regular updates. If the pace needs adjusting — faster or slower — the plan can change. The plan serves the student, not the other way round.

"Do not ask how long Hifz will take. Ask how much of the Quran you will still remember in ten years. That is the question revision answers."

What if progress feels slow?

  • Check the revision first. If old lessons are fading, the answer is usually more revision, not more new lessons. Strong revision protects everything you have already built.
  • Check the daily habit. A short practice every day matters more than a long session once a week. If practice has slipped, rebuilding the daily habit is the fastest fix.
  • Talk to the tutor. Tell them how it feels. They may reduce the new Sabaq for a while, increase revision time, or change the time of the lesson. Small adjustments often make a big difference.
  • Remember why you started. Hifz is an act of worship as well as a learning goal. Progress that feels slow on a calendar can still be deeply valuable.

Frequently asked questions

Can a child memorize the whole Quran in two years?

Some full-time students do, but most children studying part-time alongside school take several years. A two-year timeline usually needs full-time study with many hours a day. Part-time memorization over several years is normal and healthy.

Is it too late for an adult to memorize the Quran?

No. Adults memorize the Quran successfully at every age. It usually takes longer than for a child, because adults have less free time, but adults often bring stronger motivation and discipline. Many adults memorize steadily over several years.

How many days a week should a Hifz student study?

What matters more — speed or revision?

Revision, without question. Fast memorization that is forgotten is not real progress. A student who memorizes slowly but revises every day will still have their Quran years later, and that is what becoming a Hafiz really means.
